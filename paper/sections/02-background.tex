\section{Setting and Security Objects}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Text channel}
Alice and Bob share a passphrase, model and tokenizer, prompt, and decoding
configuration. Alice encrypts a plaintext, embeds the packet into generated
tokens, and transmits only their rendered text. Bob tokenizes the received text,
replays the model, and releases plaintext only after packet authentication.
A text-only passive observer sees the complete transmitted text, including its
length and endpoint, but does not obtain the passphrase, private rotation coins,
retry counters, or intermediate token annotations.

\paragraph{Observer knowledge}
Our main observer knows the model, tokenizer, candidate policy, and prompt.
This is a public-configuration threat model, not a claim about unknown-prompt
traffic. The bootstrap attack additionally uses the protocol's public 60-byte
bootstrap length. The stopping attack uses the encoded bit length $B$; this is
natural for a fixed-size application or a chosen-message security experiment.
An unknown mixture of message lengths is a separate case. We study neither
endpoint compromise nor recovery from active edits.

\paragraph{Three reference laws}
Let $P_t(\cdot\mid y_{<t})$ denote the backend's masked next-token distribution.
Truncation, tokenization filtering, and frequency quantization produce $Q_t$.
This processed source is not the unmodified model. We distinguish:
(i) a $Q$ sampler with a fixed or independently drawn output length;
(ii) a $Q$ sampler following the codec's own public state transitions and stopping
rule, including identical deterministic emissions at no-payload steps; and
(iii) ordinary model generation with its own end-of-sequence policy.
A theorem or experiment against one reference does not imply security against
the others. In particular, the coding repair below uses reference (ii), while
the stopping separation uses reference (i).

\paragraph{Distances and distinguishers}
For discrete laws $R$ and $S$, we use
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{TV}(R,S)=\tfrac12\sum_x |R(x)-S(x)|
 =\sup_A |R(A)-S(A)|.
\end{equation}
Thus an explicit event supplies a lower bound on distinguishability, regardless
of whether a trained detector finds it. Our exact examples enumerate complete
transcripts, including their termination. Local token-TV measurements describe
conditional kernels; they are not automatically distances between complete
texts. Perplexity is a fluency-related measurement, not a substitute for either
quantity.

\paragraph{Reliability and integrity}
Delivery success means that the receiver obtains exactly the original plaintext
after authentication. First-attempt success and success after retries are
reported separately. Authentication protects the recovered packet, not every
character of the visible text: Ghostext deliberately ignores trailing tokens
after packet recovery, and guarded packet-bearing paths can differ only in
discarded suffix randomness while decoding to the same packet.
Conversely, a candidate-path or interval-consistency
check may distinguish an output without any ability to authenticate it.

\paragraph{Idealizations}
The integer-code analysis assumes a uniform payload integer with independent
random guard bits. Authentication alone does not imply that a packet, including
its tags and metadata, is indistinguishable from uniform bytes; applying the
bound to encrypted traffic requires a separate fixed-length source assumption.
The stopping theorem instead uses the RRC paper's ideal independent uniform
rotations. We retain that idealization to isolate a defect that persists even
with perfect randomness. Real-model experiments specify their finite sampling
precision separately.
