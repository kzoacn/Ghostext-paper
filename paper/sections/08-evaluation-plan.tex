\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate four distinct questions: whether finite-input diagnostics describe
the executed sampling law, whether stopping creates a signal after length
matching, whether the guarded codec repairs its stated defect, and how these
mechanisms appear in a real text-channel implementation. Exact enumeration,
synthetic simulation, archived-result audit, and fresh model experiments are
kept separate. We do not turn a failed classifier or a successful decode into
an indistinguishability claim.

\subsection{Exact Coding and Stopping Audits}

The original NLS result and the RRC stopped-law table in
Sections~\ref{sec:audit}--\ref{sec:stopping} are exhaustively reproducible.
The former executes pinned original code on all 256 one-byte inputs. The latter
uses exact rational probabilities, including natural strings absent from the
stego support. Neither result is a Monte Carlo estimate.

For Ghostext, we enumerate the complete capped transcript tree for an eight-bit
uniform payload and guard precisions $g\in\{0,2,4,8,16,32,64,128\}$, with a
32-step horizon. We use three sources: uniform over 64 tokens; a three-token
source proportional to $(0.61,0.28,0.11)$ after quantization; and a
context-dependent source alternating between that table and a four-token table
according to the observed prefix. The reference follows the same public prefix
stopping rule. Zero-width reference branches and horizon exhaustion remain in
the probability space.

Table~\ref{tab:guard-tv} shows the intended effect of retaining random suffix
precision. The exact values obey Theorem~\ref{thm:guard}; the enumerator computes
them from path probabilities rather than substituting that bound. The uniform
case already reaches zero at $g=4$, because its dyadic partitions align with
payload blocks. Nonuniform tables retain finite rounding error. These are
coding-law measurements under a uniform source, not measurements of ciphertext
pseudorandomness or of ordinary natural-language security.

\subsection{Stopping with an Identical Length Histogram}

We simulate the published ideal RRC stopping law for the fixed binary source
$p=0.75$, using 20,000 outputs per class at each $B$. The natural control receives
a permutation of the stego lengths and independent fresh content, so the two
empirical length histograms are exactly equal. We fix the detector score before
sampling to $-|S(y)-B|$; there is no trained text model or fitted threshold.
Table~\ref{tab:stopping-simulation} also reports the event
$B-8\le S(y)\le B+2$, whose stopped-law probability is at least $0.984375$ by
the hazard bound. Complete simulation outputs and seeds are retained.

The growing gap is consistent with Theorem~\ref{thm:stopping}, but the theorem
is established by its proof rather than these simulations. The uniform binary
source is a negative control: its stopping time is independent of token
identities, giving exact equality with the independently length-matched law.

An independent dynamic program propagates surviving and stopped probability
mass over length and rare-symbol count. It agrees with the simulation's band
probabilities and retains total mass to within $2\cdot10^{-12}$. At $B=2048$,
the count-and-length TV is $0.915$ for $p=0.75$, whereas it is zero, up to
numerical precision, for the uniform source at every tested length. This
observable projection gives a lower bound on full ordered-transcript TV.
Figure~\ref{fig:finite-effects} separates the decreasing coding error from the
increasing stopped-text signal.

\subsection{Reanalysis of the Archived Delivery Grid}

The archive contains a fixed $10\times10$ grid: five English and five Chinese
prompts crossed with ten public messages. We verify unique pair identifiers,
cover-text hashes, packet sizes, token accounting, and the reported rate
calculation directly from the 100 records. All records report matching decoded
plaintext, but only 81 deliveries succeed on their first attempt; the 100
deliveries require 140 attempts. Failed-attempt prefixes were not retained in
that archive, so their detailed causes cannot be reconstructed from its success
summaries alone.

The fixed 88-byte framing cost is substantial for 31--147-byte plaintexts.
The mean per-text net rate is 1.172 bits/token, while aggregate plaintext bits
divided by aggregate tokens is 1.210; the two estimands differ. A prompt-cluster
bootstrap over this fixed set gives a 95\% interval of [1.127,1.215] for the
mean net rate. It describes variation over these ten prompt clusters, not
arbitrary deployment prompts. The nominal Wilson interval for 100/100 recorded
deliveries is [0.963,1], but shared grid inputs and one observation per cell limit
an independent-Bernoulli interpretation.

\subsection{Keyless Bootstrap Probe on Real Text}

We replay all 118 archived stego texts: the 100 grid records and an earlier
18-run cohort. All satisfy the legacy public bootstrap interval. For a paired
pilot, the earlier cohort also provides 18 independently sampled controls with
the same prompts and matched visible token lengths. Their historical sampler
draws from the filtered floating-point probabilities, rather than integer
frequencies $f/F$. It also emits an unfiltered raw argmax if the stable set is
empty: three such fallbacks occur across three of the 18 texts. Their positions
were not recorded. Thus this pilot does not isolate the codec against the
strict quantized source $Q$ used in the fresh experiments, and it is not an
ordinary-model-output comparison.

The legacy check accepts 18/18 stego texts and 7/18 controls; all 11 rejected
controls choose a zero-width subinterval. Thus the paired pilot has TPR 1 and
FPR 0.389, with nominal Wilson 95\% intervals [0.824,1] and [0.203,0.614],
respectively. These wide intervals and the small number of prompt families
preclude a low-FPR claim. Replaying the same texts with $g=128$ instead makes
all 36 prefixes admissible, illustrating that an interval-validity rule depends
on its precise codec state. It is not yet a measurement of newly generated
repaired traffic.

\subsection{Fresh Model Protocol}

The fresh stopping study uses ten topic families, with English contexts for
GPT-2 and paired English/Chinese contexts for Qwen. Five families are reserved
for development and five for testing before generation. Each context is
repeated eight times at 128 and 512 bits. The natural control is independently
sampled for the observed stego length under the same processed source $Q$.
The manifests specify all prompts, task order, random seeds, model hashes,
CPU affinity, and software versions. No retry silently replaces a failed
stopping-study sample.

Completion, canonical retokenization, reverse recovery, and control completion
are recorded separately. Analyses restricted to complete matched pairs must
report their denominators and excluded outcomes. The public stopping score is
computed from text-source likelihood and known $B$, without private rotations.
Additional guarded-versus-legacy delivery trials use the full authenticated
pipeline and retain every encoding attempt. Their purpose is to distinguish
coding repair from the separate stopping and retry effects, not to claim that
the repaired prototype is detector-resistant.

\subsection{Fresh Stopping Results and Selection}

The strict-source study completes all 160 planned GPT-2 pairs and 320 planned
Qwen pairs. Table~\ref{tab:stopping-outcomes} keeps attempted generation separate
from completed output. GPT-2 has 39 stego and 37 control tokenization failures;
Qwen has 19 and 5, respectively. Consequently, only 90 GPT-2 pairs and 296 Qwen
pairs satisfy the complete, canonical, exactly matched inclusion rule.
This selection is substantial for GPT-2. Reporting its denominator does not
turn a conditional detector result into an unconditional transmitted-text law.

\subsection{A Separate Source-Expansion Follow-up}

The empty-set failures motivate an explicitly different source, developed from
two failed development cases. When the original truncated pool contains no
stable token, an optional policy searches the top 4096 ranked candidates and
keeps at most 64 stable entries. Otherwise it preserves the original selection.
Both classes use this same rule. It changes the source and carries a distinct
protocol identifier; it is not a distribution-preserving transformation of
the strict source.

We retain every strict-source record and run a separate full cohort using seed
20261023, the same topic split, and the unchanged public score. All 160 GPT-2
pairs complete, with canonical text and matched lengths; four completed stego
outputs fail sender-side reverse recovery. The held-out AUCs are $0.862$ and
$0.986$ at 128 and 512 bits. Thus the measured stopping signal persists in this
follow-up without filtering out generation failures. This is a post-hoc
robustness study of the stated source, not an estimate for arbitrary prompts
or ordinary EOS generation.

\subsection{Authenticated Delivery and Coding Repair}

Fresh delivery trials cross the same prompts with two repetitions and guards
$g=0$ or $128$. Each application message is 16 UTF-8 bytes, so its packet is
104 bytes; no optional natural tail is generated. A maximum of ten attempts
is allowed, and every failed prefix is retained.

\input{tables/repair-results}

The completed GPT-2 cohort has 19/20 deliveries under each guard setting, with
12/20 first-attempt deliveries in each. Legacy and guarded trials consume 51
and 46 total attempts, respectively. Every delivery failure is due to the
strict tokenization filter, which the coding repair does not change.
The mean net rates are $0.556$ and $0.553$ bits/token; packet overhead explains
much of the difference from gross rates of $3.615$ and $3.596$.

Only nine legacy and thirteen guarded GPT-2 trials have complete matched
controls. The matching legacy bootstrap probe accepts all nine stego prefixes
and four controls. The guarded probe accepts all thirteen stego prefixes and
all thirteen controls. This removes the measured zero-width rejection signal
in that limited set. It neither rules out other distinguishers nor proves
that the entire encrypted packet satisfies the theorem's uniform-source
assumption.

\subsection{Checking the Later Stopping Modification}

A separately labelled 40-case GPT-2 ablation enables reverse verification
before accepting a stop, using the original experimental tapes. It rejects
zero candidate stops, so it is only an unchanged-path check and supplies no
evidence of a real-model recovery repair. The exact finite-tape control in the
artifact does exercise the veto and also exhibits token-budget exhaustion.

For a targeted diagnostic, we separately replay every completed reverse-failure
case from the full GPT-2 cohort. All three original token/probability sequences
reproduce exactly. Verification adds one, two, and two tokens, respectively.
An independent decoder then rebuilds intervals from visible text and the
experimental rotation tape: it fails to recover the original message before
the modification and succeeds afterward in these three cases. This selected
diagnostic establishes the behavior of our adapter on those examples, not a
general recovery rate or a security result for the current upstream software.
