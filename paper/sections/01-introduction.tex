\section{Introduction}

A language-model steganographic encoder must eventually stop. For a finite
message, a natural policy is to stop when the generated prefix lets the receiver
recover the payload. That decision depends on the sampled content: an
information-rich prefix can finish earlier than a predictable one. Even when
every active token follows the intended source distribution, the observer
receives a complete text whose content and endpoint were selected together.
Reliable recovery and accurate active sampling therefore leave a separate
security question about the transmitted text.

Arithmetic coding supplies the basic connection between random bits and model
sampling~\cite{ziegler2019neural,shen2020near}. Subsequent work strengthens security
through fresh cryptographic randomness, distribution copies, and coupling
constructions~\cite{kaptchuk2021language,ding2023discop,dewitt2024perfect}.
Finite-message granularity and rounding are already recognized
limitations~\cite{ding2023discop}. We study the additional effect of terminating
the output, giving the natural control the encoder's \emph{entire marginal
token-length distribution}, independently of fresh source content. This removes
distinguishers based only on token counts. An observer can still exploit content--length
dependence without recovering a secret or breaking a pseudorandom generator.

Our main result identifies when that residual signal grows. If an encoder finishes
in a narrow band of cumulative source information, while independently sampled
texts spread their information over a wider range, the endpoint exposes a
public signal. We prove this concentration for arithmetic-prefix recovery and
for both the published and reverse-verified rotation range-coding (RRC)
midpoint rules~\cite{yan2026rrc}. On fixed Markov sources with positive
varentropy, all three ideal mechanisms become asymptotically distinguishable
even after matching the \emph{entire marginal length distribution}. For fixed
finite-order kernels with full token support, the variance condition fails
only for uniform IID tokens. A dependent source with restricted support and
zero varentropy gives exact equality instead. A fixed
exogenous endpoint avoids the termination dependence but introduces a
quantifiable recovery-failure probability. These results concern specified
ideal mechanisms and source assumptions, which we keep separate from numerical
software measurements.

Reverse verification is a particularly important case. Current RRC code can
reject a candidate endpoint when reversing its rotations would recover the
wrong message. This changes the stopping law, so the published midpoint hazard
cannot simply be reused. We prove a new concentration bound by tracking a
one-sided interval of points that reverse safely to the original message.
A contraction argument controls unusually narrow safe intervals and shows
that correcting this recovery failure does not remove the ideal stopping
separation.

The distinction also reaches actual implementations. We execute unchanged
current RRC sender and receiver functions over full-vocabulary GPT-2 and
Qwen2.5-0.5B sources, with development and test prompt clusters and an independent
endpoint control. Public replays score every published string, including
changed tokenizations and recovery failures; a separate budget experiment
measures the recovery cost of an independent endpoint.
A public replay of Ghostext's authenticated delivery
texts checks whether their first prefix-resolution endpoint remains visible
after the integer-coding repair. These experiments keep numerical failures,
serialization changes, and delivery conditioning explicit.

Ghostext also supplies an authenticated implementation for separating the
endpoint issue from finite-input coding errors. Its original integer codec
has a terminal support restriction, addressed by a scoped guarded-prefix bound.
A separate exhaustive experiment executes the original NLS coding function:
with uniform synthetic token probabilities, finite-input padding restricts
the last token to four of 64 choices despite reported KL zero. These audits
test the executed law rather than equating a token-table diagnostic with
complete-text security.

\paragraph{Contributions}
First, we establish a stopped-transcript separation for three termination
mechanisms, including a new concentration argument for reverse verification
and nonzero finite certificates on an explicit dependent source. Second, exact external-code and
finite-integer audits distinguish padding error from rounding and endpoint
effects, with a scoped guarded-prefix repair. Third, unchanged upstream-code
experiments and keyless text replays test the protocol consequences, alongside
controlled two-model studies and complete failure records.

\paragraph{Scope}
Our results do not show that all arithmetic-coding schemes share these defects.
Meteor's fresh masking and Discop's randomized fixed-length experimental path
exclude the particular deterministic-padding example; their output policies
still require their own complete-text analysis. Nor do we claim that correcting finite-interval
rounding makes Ghostext a secure deployment tool. Filtering, retries, plaintext
length, and output stopping remain part of the observable channel. The purpose
of the revised system is to make these distinctions testable rather than to
infer complete-text security from successful decoding or token-table KL alone.
