\section{Introduction}

A language-model steganographic system must do two things that are easy to
confuse: recover a hidden message and make the transmitted text difficult to
distinguish from its cover source. Encrypting a message and mapping the resulting
bits to model tokens can address the first requirement without satisfying the
second. The distinction becomes particularly important for short, finite
messages, where packet boundaries, decoder termination, and retries determine
which texts are actually sent.

Arithmetic coding supplies the basic connection between random bits and model
sampling~\cite{ziegler2019neural,shen2020near}. Subsequent work strengthens security
through fresh cryptographic randomness, distribution copies, and coupling
constructions~\cite{kaptchuk2021language,ding2023discop,dewitt2024perfect}.
Finite-message granularity and rounding are already recognized limitations of
arithmetic-coding steganography~\cite{ding2023discop}; our starting point is not
that these limitations are new. Instead, we ask which security measurements
remain valid after a finite message is framed, encoded, and stopped.

The answer depends on the object being measured. A quantized token table may
closely approximate model probabilities, yet the encoder may select from that
table using a nonuniform finite source. Even exact next-token sampling does not
settle the distribution of complete texts when the stopping decision depends on
the sampled content. Matching the marginal length histogram removes a direct
length-only signal, but it does not remove correlations between content and
length. An observer can exploit those correlations without recovering a secret
or breaking a pseudorandom generator.

We develop this distinction through Ghostext, an authenticated linguistic
steganography prototype, and exact audits of related constructions. Our audit
finds a terminal support restriction in Ghostext's original finite-integer
codec. A separate experiment executes the original NLS coding function without
modification: under uniform synthetic token probabilities, its finite-input
padding restricts the final token to four of 64 possibilities, despite a reported
KL of zero. The mechanisms differ, so we do not attribute Ghostext's interval
implementation to NLS.

We also analyze the published rotation range-coding (RRC) algorithm of Yan and
Murawaki~\cite{yan2026rrc}. Fresh rotation preserves the next-token law during
active generation. Its message-dependent stopping rule nevertheless introduces
a public likelihood factor. We construct an exact finite counterexample and
prove an asymptotic separation from a natural sampler with the \emph{same entire
marginal length distribution}. This addresses the published stopping argument,
not the security of its random-number generator. The later implementation adds
verification before stopping; our exact formula is explicitly scoped to the
published rule.

\paragraph{Contributions}
First, we give reproducible, implementation-grounded audits separating padding,
integer rounding, and stopping leakage. Second, we derive a keyless stopping
likelihood and prove that, for a fixed nonuniform binary IID source, stopped RRC
outputs become asymptotically distinguishable from independently
length-matched natural samples. Third, we implement and analyze a guarded-prefix
codec inside an authenticated text-channel protocol. Its explicit coding bound
is complemented by raw-result reanalysis, a real-model bootstrap probe, and
controlled experiments whose reference sampling and failure handling are
specified separately.

\paragraph{Scope}
Our results do not show that all arithmetic-coding schemes share these defects.
Meteor's fresh masking and Discop's randomized fixed-length experimental path
are relevant negative controls. Nor do we claim that correcting finite-interval
rounding makes Ghostext a secure deployment tool. Filtering, retries, plaintext
length, and output stopping remain part of the observable channel. The purpose
of the revised system is to make these distinctions testable rather than to
infer complete-text security from successful decoding or token-table KL alone.
